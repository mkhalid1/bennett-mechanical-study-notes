# How BMCT-II scoring works

Short version: the BMCT-II does not simply count your correct answers. Because every candidate gets a different set of items, the scoring system estimates your ability from which items you got right and how hard they were, then reports it as a percentile against a comparison group the employer chooses. There is no published pass mark.

Everything on this page about how the test is scored comes from [Pearson TalentLens's BMCT-II page and FAQ](https://www.talentlens.com/bennett-mechanical.html), checked 24 November 2026. Where something is our own advice, we say so.

## Why raw scores are not enough

On a fixed test, everyone sees the same questions, so the number correct tells you a lot. The BMCT-II is different. Pearson draws each candidate's 55 items at random from an item bank, with the same number of items at each difficulty level and the same mix of content. Even so, Pearson says the sets "may differ slightly in difficulty overall".

So Pearson says the number of correct answers, the raw score, should not be used on its own to make hiring decisions. Two people with 40 correct could have faced slightly different sets.

## The theta score

Instead, the scoring system uses the exact difficulty of every item you answered to estimate your ability. This estimate is called a **theta score**, or ability score. Getting harder items right counts for more.

Pearson says theta scores appear in the employer's online score dashboard, and that theta is converted to a percentile for interpretation.

## Percentile and norm groups

A **percentile** compares you with a **norm group**: a large sample of people who have taken the test, such as applicants for a certain kind of job. If you are at the 70th percentile, you scored the same as or higher than 70% of that group.

Pearson says administrators are encouraged to pick the most appropriate norm group when they assign the test, and that a completed test can be rescored against a different norm group through the platform.

What this means for you: the same performance can give different percentiles depending on the comparison group. You cannot work out your percentile from a practice score, because you do not know which norm group your employer uses.

## What the report shows

According to Pearson, the employer's Profile Report includes an analysis of your results, and its Additional Technical Information section reports:

| Score type | What it is |
|---|---|
| Number correct | Your raw score. Reported, but Pearson advises against using it alone. |
| T-score | A standard score with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 |
| Stanine | A nine-point standard scale, 1 to 9, with 5 in the middle |
| Sten | A ten-point standard scale, 1 to 10, with 5.5 in the middle |
| Percentile | The share of the norm group you matched or beat |

Pearson recommends that employers use the percentile rank or a standardised score, because those take account of differences in item difficulty. Whether you see any of this yourself depends on the employer.

The T-score, stanine and sten descriptions above are the standard psychometric definitions, in our own words.

## Is there a pass mark?

Pearson does not publish one. Each employer decides what level it needs for the role, often as a minimum percentile, and that may differ between roles in the same company. Numbers quoted online as "the Bennett pass mark" are not from Pearson. Treat them as guesses.

If you want to know where you stand, ask the employer or training programme what they look for. Some will tell you. Many will not.

## Is there a penalty for guessing?

Pearson does not publish a penalty for wrong answers. Our advice: answer every item. A blank scores nothing, while a guess between three options has a fair chance of being right, and a guess after ruling out one option has an even better one.

## What counts as a good score

Since the norm group and the cut-off are both set by the employer, nobody outside can tell you what score you need. What you can control:

- **Aim for accuracy on the easy items.** Every candidate's set includes easy items. Dropping them through rushing hurts most.
- **Cover every topic.** Each test includes a set amount of each content type, so a weak topic cannot be avoided.
- **Aim high on practice.** On timed, well-made practice tests, aim to answer around 45 of 55 correctly, about 80%, with time to spare. That leaves a margin for nerves and for harder items on the day. This is our study guidance, not a published standard.

## Why your score can vary between attempts

You will see a different set of items each time. The scoring system adjusts for difficulty, but small swings between attempts are normal. Whether you can retake the test, and how soon, is up to the employer. Pearson does not publish a retake rule for candidates.
